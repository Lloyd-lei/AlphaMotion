I set in advance for "body-specific". So the headline test is INCONCLUSIVE, and
neither explanation wins outright. But the per-cell numbers say more than the
summary does:

 * KICK is uniformly hard: CV 0.367, and it is the worst or near-worst family on
   every single robot (9.0-26.3%). Nothing about a particular body explains it.
   This is the profile of a DATA problem (or a systematic retarget problem that
   affects all bodies equally).
 * JACKS is the opposite and it is extreme: CV 1.144, ranging from 1.11 on
   hightorque_hi to 17.86 on g1_29dof — a 16x spread. Jumping jacks are nearly
   free on some bodies and catastrophic on others. That is a BODY-SPECIFIC
   signature, i.e. infeasibility, and no amount of extra data fixes the bodies
   that cannot do it.
 * PUNCH sits in between (CV 0.573), with kuavo_s45 (27.5%) far worse than the
   rest — again a body effect on top of a family effect.
 * Two robots are consistently the worst across every family: unitree_g1_29dof
   and kuavo_s45. G1 is a HELD-OUT embodiment (never trained on), so its
   elevated errors are expected and should be read separately; kuavo_s45 is
   trained, so its across-the-board weakness is its own finding.

VERDICT: "our data is too small" is PARTIALLY supported and must not be acted
on as a single explanation.
  - For kick (the worst family), the uniform profile is consistent with more
    data helping.
  - For jacks, and partly punch, the variation is body-specific and more data
    will not help those cells at all.
  - The frequency correlation (-0.684) that motivated this test is real but
    confounded: run is only 7.3% of the corpus and is the BEST family, which
    already showed frequency is not sufficient on its own.
RECOMMENDATION BEFORE BUYING DATA: buy for the families whose profile is
uniform (kick first), and fix retargeting or change the yardstick for the ones
whose profile is body-specific (jacks). Buying broadly on the strength of the
-0.684 alone would spend money on cells that cannot improve.

VERDICT: HYPOTHESIS B IS REFUTED AS A FAMILY-LEVEL EXPLANATION. Hard and easy
families sit on the stops at the same rate (4.88% vs 4.98%). KICK, the worst
family of all, has the LOWEST saturation of any family (2.73%) — the motion the
model fails hardest on is the one least constrained by the robots' limits. So
the arm tail is not the joints running out of travel.

TWO THINGS THE TABLE DOES EXPLAIN
 * The jacks 16x cell IS a limit story after all, just not a family-level one:
   jacks on g1_29dof saturates 10.39% of limited DOFs, on hightorque_hi 0.00% —
   exactly the two ends of the 17.86 vs 1.11 error spread. So for THAT cell,
   more data will not help; the body cannot make the pose.
 * kuavo_s45 saturates 6.8-23.1% on EVERY family, far above every other body.
   Its across-the-board weakness in the previous table is now explained: its
   descriptor's limits are being hit constantly, on walking as much as on
   punching. That is a per-robot data/descriptor problem worth fixing on its
   own, and it has been contaminating every cross-embodiment average we report.

WHERE THE ARM TAIL NOW STANDS, after four eliminations
  decoder capacity        refuted (two post-trains, <1%)
  loss wiring             refuted (FK loss, ~2.5%)
  code budget             refuted (rot_only 128 vs 256, 1-4%)
  joint-limit clamping    refuted (this table, ratio 0.98)
  family rarity           partially supported (corr -0.684) but confounded, and
                          contradicted by run (7.3% of the corpus, best family)
Still standing and now the leading candidate: the retargeting labels themselves
carry family-dependent error that is not about limits — i.e. the solver does
worse on kick/punch/jacks for reasons other than running out of joint travel.
The readout probe already showed the ceiling is in the labels (codes -> paired
wrist saturates ~30 cm while codes -> human wrist reaches 12 cm). This table
says that ceiling is not made of joint stops.
